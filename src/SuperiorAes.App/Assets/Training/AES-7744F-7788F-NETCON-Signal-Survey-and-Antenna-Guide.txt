1. Scope, safety and acceptance target
This guide is for diagnosing persistent NETCON 6 or 7 on AES IntelliNet 7744F and 7788F legacy fire subscribers. It uses the
subscriber's Local Status, Routing Table, RX Monitor, TX Monitor and Monitor All functions together with controlled
antenna-location and RF-path tests.
Fire-system target
The legacy subscriber manuals describe NETCON 0-6 as generally usable and 7 as a possible problem, but require fire
installations to achieve NETCON 0-5. Current AES antenna-placement guidance likewise targets NETCON 5 for fire. A
constant 6 or 7 on a 7744F/7788F fire subscriber requires investigation.
Safety requirements
- Notify the central station and place the account on test before reset, keying or event tests.
 
- Never key the transmitter without a correct 50-ohm antenna, dummy load or approved RF test instrument connected.
 
- Keep antennas and installation equipment away from electrical conductors. AES recommends at least 10 feet from external
 power lines.
- Use fall protection, lightning safety and qualified personnel for rooftop work.
 
- Remote outdoor antennas require approved surge protection, weatherproofing, grounding and code-compliant installation.
 
- Do not change subscriber frequency, power, cipher or network engineering parameters without authorization.

Guide sequence
Do not begin by installing the largest antenna. First determine whether the weakness is local RF, the feed line, interference,
configuration or the upstream mesh. Then change one variable at a time and record the result after the routing table has had
time to rebuild.

2. NETCON is not a signal-strength meter
NETCON is the subscriber's internal Network Connectivity rating. It reflects confidence in available paths through the mesh, not
a direct measurement in dBm. Antenna work can improve NETCON when local RF links are poor, but an antenna cannot repair
unhealthy routes farther upstream.

Why a good antenna can still leave NETCON at 6
- The best-heard neighbors may themselves have high NETCON or high link levels.
 
- All usable neighbors may depend on the same upstream path, providing little route diversity.
 
- A neighboring repeater may have a fault, weak onward RF path or be leaving the network.
 
- The local unit may hear packets well but acknowledgements do not reliably return.
 
- Mesh density near the site may be inadequate even though one strong peer is audible.
 
Core diagnostic rule
Use Q to judge the local RF link to each routing-table peer. Use the peer's N and L values to judge the quality of the route
beyond that peer. Use packet acknowledgements to confirm end-to-end behavior.

7. Isolate antenna/feed line, transceiver or network signal
Use controlled substitution. Change only one part at a time and use the same location, wait interval and routing-table capture. A
continuity test alone cannot prove that an antenna or coax works correctly at RF.

A comparison transceiver must be authorized, frequency-compatible and connected under central-station test procedures.
Do not introduce a duplicate active subscriber ID. Record which board, transceiver, ID and antenna are used for every
trial.
Antenna or feed-line fault signature
- Forward power is normal into a known-good dummy load.
 
- Reflected power is excessive only when the installed feed line/antenna is connected.
 
- A known-good short cable and matched antenna produce better Q and NETCON at the same location.
 
- Results change when a connector or cable is moved.
 
- Water, corrosion, loose connectors, braid shorts, crushed coax or an incorrect-frequency antenna is found.
 
- The case-top antenna works, but adding the remote coax/surge/antenna makes the routing table worse.
 
Transceiver or power fault signature
- Forward power is low into a known-good 50-ohm load.
 
- Supply voltage drops abnormally while e keys the transmitter.
 
- Loopback/status fault 080 persists after reset.
 
- With the same known-good antenna and location, a compatible known-good radio hears substantially more IDs or better Q.
 
- Local TX packets repeatedly receive no P_ACK while a comparison unit at the same location succeeds.
 
- Transmit power can be normal while receiver sensitivity is poor; compare Monitor All and routing tables between radios.

8. Controlled site-survey procedure
Phase A - outside free-air reference
Before committing to an indoor location, establish whether viable mesh coverage exists outside the facility. AES notes that a
poor result outside will not improve inside the building.
- Use the correct subscriber ID and network cipher.
 
- Begin with the case-top/rubber-duck antenna or a 7214-EMK test mount.
 
- Keep the temporary antenna clear of vehicles, large metal surfaces and the technician's body.
 
- Reset after every move and allow up to 5 minutes for the final routing table and NETCON.
 
- Record d status, the complete Ctrl+T table and a short a/c Monitor All sample.
 
- If NETCON 5 cannot be reached outside, test an approved higher-gain antenna before quoting a normal indoor installation.
 
Phase B - indoor candidate locations
Use the 7214-EMK with the rubber-duck antenna to create the needed mounting/radial condition and move the antenna through
candidate locations using an appropriate 6-, 10- or 25-foot test cable.
- Test higher locations first.
 
- Prefer an outside wall and a clear view toward the expected network coverage area.
 
- Avoid basements, below-grade rooms, elevator equipment, large metal cabinets and metal roof shielding.
 
- Keep the antenna separated from electrical wiring, motors, transformers and switching power equipment.
 
- Reset after each move. Do not compare a newly moved antenna against a previous fully developed table.

9. Antenna placement
Indoor placement
- Use the case-top antenna where it produces compliant and stable routing.
 
- Use a proper mounting kit/ground-plane arrangement when remotely testing a rubber-duck antenna.
 
- Move higher and toward an outside wall before increasing antenna gain.
 
- Keep the radiating element away from large grounded metal, electrical wiring and noise sources.
 
- Do not coil excess coax beside the antenna or sharply bend/crush the feed line.
 
Outdoor placement
Current AES placement guidance recommends an unobstructed view of the intended coverage area, ideally 360 degrees. The
antenna should be plumb and clear of walls, parapets and roof structures.

Why too much gain can be harmful
Higher-gain omnidirectional antennas concentrate energy into a flatter vertical pattern. They can hear/transmit farther while
reducing useful close-in vertical coverage, creating overreach and allowing distant traffic to dominate. Select gain from
route-table evidence, not from the largest available dB number.

Inspection checklist
- Correct 50-ohm cable type and connector family.
 
- No sharp bends, crushing, staples through the jacket or pulled center conductor.
 
- No loose adapters, damaged threads, braid shorts or incomplete crimps.
 
- No water, green corrosion or blackened connector surfaces.
 
- Cable shield and center conductor pass continuity tests and are not shorted together.
 
- Feed line is not longer than necessary.
 
Outdoor installation
- Use AES 7230 or the currently approved surge protector for every remote outdoor antenna.
 
- Install grounding and bonding according to AES instructions, NEC and local code.
 
- Protect threaded connections with suitable dielectric grease.
 
- Seal exposed connectors with approved mastic/self-fusing weatherproofing tape.
 
- Provide drip loops and physical protection where required.
 
Loss-versus-gain reality
A higher-gain antenna at the end of excessive or defective coax can perform worse than a smaller antenna on a short feed.
Compare the routing table before and after adding the complete cable, surge protector and adapters - not the antenna alone.
Useful isolation test
If a case-top antenna works but the remote antenna does not, temporarily substitute a known-good short cable and
antenna. Then add the permanent feed-line sections one at a time to locate the loss.

When to escalate to the network administrator
- Multiple local Q03 routes exist but local NETCON remains 6/7.
 
- Every viable peer shows high N or high L values.
 
- The same weak upstream ID appears in many affected subscribers.
 
- A subscriber has only one viable route even in clear outdoor conditions.
 
- Packet acknowledgements are inconsistent across multiple known-good local installations.
 
- A frequency/interference survey suggests a network-wide channel problem.
 
Information to provide
- Subscriber ID, model, firmware, location and antenna/coax configuration.
 
- Status output at 1, 3 and 5 minutes after reset.
 
- Complete routing tables from inside, outside and final antenna location.
 
- Monitor lines showing packet numbers, FROM/TO IDs, retries and acknowledgements.
 
- Forward/reflected-power measurements and supply voltage under key.
 
- Photographs of antenna height, clearance, cable, surge protector and connectors.
 
Constant NETCON 6/7 with good Q is data
Do not discard this evidence. A full table of Q03 neighbors with poor N/LEVEL values demonstrates that the antenna is
hearing well and the remaining problem is likely network topology or upstream health.

Guidance differences
AES web guidance published at different times gives both minimum and preferred roof-clearance figures, and older
articles mention LMR-400 runs up to 125 feet. This guide uses the stricter current placement recommendation and the
Antenna Matrix recommendation of 100 feet or less. Follow the latest AES engineering direction for the specific
installation.
